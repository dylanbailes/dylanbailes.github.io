From a single stimulus chamber to a research platform

Our sponsor's earlier bioreactor applied fields to a single cell-culture chamber. The next version needed multiple chambers, independent electric and magnetic stimuli, fluid access, and microscope imaging. We built the Multi-Chamber Camera Bioreactor (MCCB) as a four-person UC San Diego MAE 156B capstone team in spring 2026.

The final report records team spending of $3,185.38 against a $3,600 budget. The system was designed for four optical stations, but one complete station was installed within the project scope.

The engineering challenge was to fit the field generators, clear-bottom wells, electrodes, sensors, and optics into repeatable modules that a researcher could operate from one touchscreen.

What I owned

I took the magnetic subsystem from geometry selection through four physical iterations, custom board design, and bench validation. I also contributed the initial electric-field simulations, evaluated lid concepts, and completed the final interface design and testing.

A well module with room for optics

Each clear-bottom well sits inside a Helmholtz coil pair. Titanium plates apply the electric stimulus; the threaded lid provides fluid ports and illumination. A Hall-sensor satellite fits around the well, and the microscope observes the sample from below. The extrusion frame supports four modules and a Raspberry Pi touchscreen.

The final control path used ESP32 controllers and perfboard drive electronics. The four-station imaging layout was part of the design, while the delivered hardware contained one complete optical station.

Choose interfaces that can be assembled and serviced

My individual design analysis compared an adhesive-sealed Delrin concept, a serviceable acrylic lid with threaded/compression fittings, and a spring-loaded PTFE concept with Luer/PEEK connections. I weighed sealing, chemical compatibility, deflection, assembly effort, cost, and lead time.

I recommended the serviceable middle option at that stage. The team's final design evolved to a resin-printed threaded well and lid with an O-ring, Luer fluid connections, a central LED diffuser, and bolted electrode ring terminals. The concept recommendation and the delivered geometry were different stages of the design process.

Four iterations of the magnetic generator

I first compared an air-gap horseshoe magnet with a Helmholtz pair. The horseshoe offered lower current demand, but the analysis showed larger lateral field variation and raised concerns about residual magnetism. The Helmholtz configuration offered a more uniform central region and a practical path to alternating fields.

An early coil-spacing error reduced the field substantially. Correcting the geometry so coil separation equaled the radius was a key change. Later iterations improved well access, mounting, and sensor-board integration.

The final coil used 156 turns of 24 AWG wire per coil, a 27 mm radius, and 27 mm separation, with a printed ASA housing. The report predicts approximately 5 mT at 1 A and compares that prediction with a measured field map.

Two board designs and a working delivery path

I designed a main drive PCB and a separate Hall-sensor satellite in KiCad. The drive board combined the STM32 control path with field-drive electronics; the satellite positioned two Hall sensors close to the well. Mechanical packaging was part of the board design, rather than an afterthought.

The custom main board did not reach full integration within the schedule. The team delivered stimulus control using ESP32/perfboard assemblies while retaining the satellite board for magnetic sensing. That gave us a working system to test and a specific board revision to pursue.

Separate waveform timing from commands and sensing

My STM32 prototype work included peripheral bring-up, I²C/UART interfaces, option-byte recovery, and direct register configuration where needed. The final available control firmware uses an ESP32 with a DRV8876 H-bridge, 20 kHz PWM, and Hall-sensor readout.

The firmware assigns waveform generation to one ESP32 core and serial commands/sensing to the other. It supports off, DC step, square, sine, and triangle commands, plus Hall zeroing and telemetry. The interface's calibration sequence relates PWM duty to measured field so the user can request a field value.

Delivered behavior: calibrated waveform drive and Hall telemetry. The supplied FullModule.ino removes current monitoring and does not implement a completed closed-loop field regulator. Closed-loop control, automatic thermal protection, and final STM32 board integration remain further work.

Use analysis to choose geometry, then test it

I contributed initial electric-field simulations and magnetic-field analysis to guide geometry selection. The team later revised the electric-field models for the final well. Simulation helped us reason about field distribution, electrode geometry, and packaging before committing to hardware.

Make the hardware usable from one screen

The final report credits me with final UI design and testing, building on Kaitlyn's initial interface and Samantha's camera integration. The Raspberry Pi interface provides device assignment, firmware initialization, per-well parameters, calibration, live magnetic readings, and camera controls.

The demo walks through assigning controllers, calibrating PWM against Hall readings, selecting waveform and field settings, and adjusting exposure, gain, and frame rate. The camera screen supports a four-view layout, while the delivered prototype had one installed camera station.

What I would carry into the next build

  • Check geometry before increasing power. Coil spacing affected the field enough that a geometry correction mattered more than simply driving harder.
  • Package the sensing path with the mechanism. Well access, optical clearance, wire routing, and the Hall-board outline all shaped the magnetic assembly.
  • Keep a working integration path. The ESP32/perfboard implementation let the team demonstrate the system while the custom drive board needed revision.
  • Calibrate the user command against a measurement. Duty-cycle percentage alone does not describe the field at the sample.
  • Extend validation beyond a successful demo. The next tests should quantify cross-well interaction, temperature at the sample, long-duration stability, and repeatability before biological experiments.

The next hardware revision would complete the main board, add verified current and thermal protection, and integrate closed-loop field regulation. Additional optical stations and sustained culture testing would then establish the platform's full experimental scope.
